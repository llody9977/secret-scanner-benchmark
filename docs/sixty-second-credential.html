The Sixty‑Second Credential

A publicly exposed, harvestable credential can be exploited within a minute. That is far faster than human revocation typically runs. That timing, and not the detection rate, decides what secret scanning can and cannot do for you.

Part 1 of 4 · Threat and business case Research current to August 2026 ~6,000 words · 27 min

Executive summary

  1. Secret exposure is an access path, not a hygiene problem (a working route into cloud estates, CI/CD pipelines and SaaS data). MFA does not gate it: MFA is enforced at an interactive sign-in, and a leaked key, token or session cookie is presented on API calls that have no sign-in step. Possession is the whole of the authentication. The exceptions (step-up authentication, continuous access evaluation, sender-constrained tokens) have to be deployed deliberately, and tier one below is largely about buying them.
  2. Volume is rising sharply. GitGuardian counted roughly 28.65 million hardcoded secrets added to public GitHub in 2025, up 34 percent year on year, with AI-service credentials up 81 percent. 1
  3. Automation has compressed the attacker's timeline to near zero. Industry reporting puts the median from a secret hitting a public repository to first attacker use at under sixty seconds,2 and a controlled honeypot measured first abuse at about a minute. 3
  4. AI has increased the supply of secrets, though the headline figure is narrower than it is usually quoted: 3.2 percent of Claude Code-assisted commits leaked a secret against a 1.5 percent baseline (one assistant, one vendor's telemetry, uncontrolled). 1 Section two has what that does and does not support.
  5. Placement decides whether scanning prevents or merely observes. Pre-commit and pre-receive scanning are preventive; post-commit scanning is detective. The category is routinely sold as though all of it prevented. Section one is the full taxonomy, and the evidence for each part's limits.
  6. The decisive failure is remediation, not detection. GitGuardian found 64 percent of secrets confirmed valid in 2022 still valid in January 2026.1 Detection without revocation produces a finding, not a risk reduction.
  7. A large and rising share of exposure originates outside your own pipeline: Verizon found third-party involvement in 48 percent of breaches, up from 30.5 Common and growing (not a prediction about your worst incident).
  8. The controls that move risk make the secret worthless: short-lived and workload-bound credentials, automated revocation, and a rotation SLA with a named owner. Scanning tells you where to point them.
  9. There is an obligation case, and it is narrower than it is sold. No framework requires a product called a secret scanner. They require outcomes. Three name hardcoded secrets in normative text, each scope-limited (PCI DSS 8.6.2, OWASP ASVS 13.3.1, NIST SP 800-218A); ISO 27001, SOC 2, base NIST SSDF, CIS and DORA reach it by inference.
The shape of the problem

Three numbers that set the strategy

Attacker clock <60seconds Reported median from a secret appearing in a public repository to first use by an attacker. 2
Defender clock 27days Self-reported average time to remediate a leaked secret, from a survey of 1,000 IT decision-makers. 17
Revocation gap 64% Share of secrets confirmed valid in 2022 that GitGuardian found still valid in January 2026, roughly four years unrevoked. A validity measurement at one observation point, not a claim that they will never be revoked. Note also what the denominator is not documented to be: whether it covers every secret detected in 2022 or only those the vendor could still re-validate four years later. Those are different populations and the second would be the more favourable one. 1

Those three figures are the argument. The attacker operates in seconds because discovery is automated. The defender operates in weeks because remediation is human. And the credential does not expire on its own, so the gap between the two clocks never closes without someone closing it.

A caveat on comparing them. These three numbers come from different populations measured in different ways, and none is a like-for-like counterpart of another. The sixty-second figure is a median across credentials that reached a public surface, where automated harvesting applies; it says nothing about a secret in a private repository. The 27-day figure is self-reported by decision-makers about their own organisations, and "remediate" is their word, not necessarily "revoked at the provider." The 64 percent is a longitudinal validity check on one vendor's historical detections, and the published figure does not state whether the denominator is all 2022 detections or only those still re-validatable in 2026. A difference that would change what the number means.

One comparison in particular is worth refusing. IBM's widely quoted 247-day mean time to identify and contain a breach6 describes a different process: it averages across organisations that had not yet discovered they were breached. Once a scanner has fired, discovery has already happened, and the relevant interval is detection-to-revocation (hours or days in a competent programme, not months). Setting 247 days against sixty seconds makes the rhetoric stronger and the analysis worse.

One more caveat, on the largest number of the three. A count of detected secrets is not a measure of risk. Twenty-eight million leaked strings and twenty-eight million exploitable footholds are very different quantities, and the gap between them is everything this article otherwise argues matters: whether the credential is still valid, what privilege it carries, whether it is unique or one value repeated across a thousand forks, how long it was exposed, whether the system it opens is reachable from where the credential was found, and whether it clusters with other credentials into a usable path. Volume is the number that is visible, because detection is what vendors instrument; it is a proxy for risk and a weak one. It is used here to establish that the problem is large and growing, which it can support, and not to size the exposure, which it cannot. Where this article later argues for measuring revocation and validity instead of detection counts, that argument applies to its own opening figures too. What survives the caveats is the shape rather than the ratio: automated exploitation is measured in seconds, human remediation in days to weeks, and a large fraction of credentials are still valid years after they leaked. Every control decision below follows from that shape. A control that shaves days off the defender's clock cannot prevent the first use. That race is lost in the first minute, and no improvement to detection or triage latency wins it. It can still matter a great deal afterwards: cutting exposure from twenty-seven days to two days cuts the window for persistence, repeated use, lateral movement and quiet data egress by roughly an order of magnitude, and most of the damage in the case studies below accrued after the first use, not during it. The distinction worth holding is between controls that reduce the blast radius of an exposure and controls that reduce its probability. Faster revocation is the first kind and is worth investing in. Only the second kind (shortening the credential's useful life, constraining what it can do, or preventing the push in the first place) changes whether the first use happens at all.

Section one · The honest verdict

Is secret scanning actually effective?

The claim worth testing is that secret scanning is a preventive measure that does not work very well. The evidence supports a more precise version. But getting to it requires first separating five things that the word "scanning" is routinely used to cover.

What each part of a secrets programme actually is
ActivityControl typeWhat it can honestly claim
Pre-commit and pre-receive scanningPreventiveThe secret never reaches the remote, bounded by "of the types the ruleset covers."
Post-commit scanningDetectiveThe secret is already there. This tells you about it.
Validity checkingPrioritisationRanks findings. Neither prevents nor fixes anything.
Revocation and rotationCorrectiveThe only step that actually removes the exposure.
Short-lived, scoped, workload-bound identityPreventive architectureReduces what a stolen credential is worth, and for how long.

With that taxonomy in place the argument is narrower and harder to dismiss as positioning against the scanning market. Pre-receive scanning genuinely prevents. Post-commit scanning genuinely detects. The category is marketed as though the second inherited the properties of the first. And the great majority of deployed scanning, including every scheduled full-history scan and every CI job that runs after the push, is the second kind.

Start with what is genuinely good. Detection quality can be high (in Basak and colleagues' peer-reviewed comparison of nine tools at North Carolina State University, GitHub's scanner achieved 75 percent precision against 46 percent for the next-best tool evaluated). 14 Push protection, enabled by default on public repositories, blocks credentials before they ever reach a commit, which is real prevention at that one boundary. GitHub reported 39 million secret-scanning findings across the platform in 2024.15 Read that as detections, not as 39 million exploitable exposures (the same distinction this article insists on for leak volume applies here). What it establishes is the scale at which the platform control operates, not the risk it removed. 15 None of that is nothing.

Then look at the four places the model breaks.

Gap 1
Coverage

High-precision detection depends on recognisable token formats. Generic secrets (database connection strings, internal service passwords, bespoke API keys) have no distinctive shape, and by GitGuardian's measurement made up around two-thirds of detections. 16 Detector counts differ materially between tools, and only a subset of any tool's detectors are wired into push protection.

Meanwhile GitGuardian found 28 percent of incidents originate entirely outside repositories (in Slack, Jira and Confluence) and secrets found in collaboration tools were 13 percentage points more likely to be critical. Internal repositories were roughly six times more likely than public ones to contain hardcoded secrets, and receive far less scrutiny. 1

Gap 2
Timing

Post-commit scanning is a detection control racing an adversary who wins in under a minute. Even instantaneous detection and a perfect alert leaves the credential exploitable, because the human steps that follow take hours. For any secret that reaches a public surface, the correct assumption is that it is already compromised at the moment of detection.

Gap 3
Bypass and blind spots

Push protection is real-time only: GitGuardian's analysis notes it does not scan history, and does not re-evaluate a private repository that is later made public; very large pushes can exceed the scan window; and new service token formats are undetected until a detector is written for them. 16 Developers can also bypass a block by declaring a false positive or a test credential (logged and notified, but permitted).

Gap 4
Remediation

This is the one that decides the verdict. GitGuardian found 64 percent of secrets confirmed valid in 2022 were still valid in January 2026, and roughly 70 percent were still live in early 2025.1 The finding was generated; the credential was not revoked. Prioritising by validity alone does not rescue this either (the same research found that approach missed 46 percent of critical secrets).

Verdict

Pre-receive scanning is a real preventive control, bounded by its ruleset. Everything downstream of the push is detective, and the market sells the whole category on the first one's properties. Detection reliably tells you what leaked; on its own it does nothing about the exposure, because only revocation removes it and the credential stays valid until someone acts.

The practical implication is a budget one. Spending more on better detection produces more findings against a remediation pipeline that already fails to close two-thirds of them. Marginal return is higher in pushing scanning earlier (to the pre-receive boundary, where it is genuinely preventive) and in revocation automation and credential lifetime, than in widening detection coverage.

Much of the leak-volume and validity data in this article comes from GitGuardian, which sells a secret-detection platform, and its measurement of the problem is not disinterested. The specific numbers should be treated as directional. The structural argument does not depend on them: it depends on the fact that a valid credential does not expire, which is a property of the credential, not of anyone's telemetry.

Section two · Trend

AI changed both sides of the ledger

The convenient story is that AI helps attackers. The accurate story splits into two movements usually run together. AI has increased the number of secrets in circulation. Measurable, and the figures are below. The time taken to find them was already close to zero before language models existed: continuous harvesting and automated validation have operated at scale for years, and neither requires a model. What compounds is more secrets meeting older automation, not AI closing the discovery gap. And the second half of that story is where most of the overclaiming happens.

Supply: more secrets, in more places

Public GitHub absorbed about 28.65 million hardcoded secrets in 2025 according to GitGuardian, a 34 percent increase on the prior year and the largest single-year jump it has recorded. 1 That growth is partly a denominator effect, and it is worth being precise about it. GitGuardian reports public commit volume rose about 43 percent and the active developer base about 33 percent over the same period, which implies the per-commit leak rate actually fell slightly, from roughly 1.6 percent to about 1.5 percent.

That last calculation is mine, derived from three published aggregates rather than reported directly, and it matters for an honest reading: the industry is not getting dramatically worse at avoiding secrets in code. It is producing far more code. Rate improvements of a few percent are being swamped by volume, and risk is a function of volume.

Three things are new in the composition of that volume, all from the same GitGuardian dataset:1

  • AI service credentials grew 81 percent to about 1.28 million detected leaks in 2025, including roughly 113,000 DeepSeek API keys. Eight of the ten fastest-growing detector categories tie to AI services. LLM supporting infrastructure (orchestration, retrieval, vector stores) leaked about five times faster than the model providers themselves.
  • One coding assistant leaked at roughly twice the baseline rate, and the scope of that finding is the whole of it. GitGuardian measured Claude Code-assisted commits, within its own detection dataset, at 3.2 percent against a 1.5 percent baseline across all public GitHub commits. Not a controlled comparison, not a measurement of AI-generated code generally, and not evidence about any other assistant. As a signal that assistant-authored commits deserve their own risk treatment it is useful; as "AI code leaks twice as much" it is overstated. The Cloud Security Alliance separately found AI-generated code introducing security defects in about 45 percent of tasks. General vulnerabilities, not secrets, so it sits alongside this figure rather than supporting it. 4
  • New file types nobody governs. Model Context Protocol configuration files alone accounted for 24,008 unique exposed secrets, of which 2,117 were still valid. These files sit on developer workstations and in agent deployments, largely outside the repository scanning perimeter that most programs are built around.

Demand: discovery is instant, and what follows is automated

Automated harvesting of public commit streams is not new, and it is worth separating what is well evidenced here from what is merely asserted, because the AI framing is doing more work in the trade press than the evidence supports.

Well evidenced. Harvesting and credential validation run continuously at scale, and have for years. The Cloud Security Alliance's tracking of one credential-theft operation between December 2025 and January 2026 catalogued around 35,000 attack sessions, averaging 972 per day, with automated scanning for exposed endpoints and validation before resale. 20 None of that requires a language model.

Also well evidenced, and newer. LLM agents are now demonstrably operating post-exploitation at machine speed. The same research documents an incident in May 2026 in which an agent made twelve API calls across eleven distinct points of presence in a 22-second burst and completed a full database dump in under two minutes. 20 That is what an attacker does after a credential works.

Asserted rather than demonstrated. The specific claim (repeated widely, including in the industry analysis this article cites for the sixty-second figure) is that a model now reasons about each harvested key: what it unlocks, whether it is live, what privilege it carries, and therefore what to act on first. 2 That is plausible, and it is consistent with the two evidenced facts above. I could not find a primary source documenting it as an observed capability. Treat it as a characterisation of where this is going, not as a measured one; the timing data below stands on its own without it.

The consequence shows in the timings. Comparitech's honeypot research placed first abuse of a deliberately leaked AWS key at about one minute after publication, with over 1,000 RunInstances calls in the following minute and 547 distinct source addresses participating; most activity landed within the first hour. 3 Current industry reporting puts the median public-repository-to-first-use interval under sixty seconds. 2

Read that against the defender's loop. A scanner detects the commit, raises an alert, a triage queue receives it, someone identifies the owning team, the owner confirms the credential is real, and only then is it revoked. Even an excellent programme measures that in hours. The credential was used in the first minute.

Section three · Boundaries

Three exposures, three different roles for a secret

These three were selected not because they were the largest, but because each shows a secret playing a structurally different role in the attack. Treating them as one category ("leaked credentials") is what causes programmes to buy one control and assume it covers all three.

Role 1 · Secret as pivot

Salesloft Drift → Salesforce (UNC6395)

Per Mandiant's investigation as reported by TechTarget, attackers held access to Salesloft's GitHub repositories from as early as March 2025, then pivoted into the Drift application's AWS environment and took OAuth tokens issued for Drift customers' integrations. Those tokens were then used to query customers' Salesforce instances directly. 78

What matters is what they did next. Google's Threat Intelligence Group reported that they ran an automated tool across the stolen data hunting further secrets (AWS keys and Snowflake tokens embedded in support cases and CRM records). 7 A support ticket where a customer pasted a key in 2023 becomes an access path in 2025.

Business impact. Drift was taken offline entirely from early September 2025 while the vendor rebuilt it; Salesforce suspended the integration. 9 Named affected organisations included Cloudflare, Palo Alto Networks, Zscaler, CyberArk, JFrog and PagerDuty8 (each absorbing unplanned incident response, mass rotation, customer notification and regulatory assessment, for a breach in a chatbot vendor they had onboarded as a marketing tool).

The lesson. These were valid OAuth bearer tokens. MFA was satisfied upstream, when a customer administrator authorised the integration, and presenting the token never re-triggers it. So password policy and MFA strength had no opportunity to act. Only integration scoping, token lifetime and anomaly detection on token use were in a position to change the outcome.

Role 2 · Secret as payload

Shai-Hulud 2.0: the self-propagating npm worm

This is the case where secrets are not the way in. They are the objective. Per Sysdig's analysis, the worm ran during preinstall and harvested credentials from every direction at once: cloud SDK modules, environment variables, .npmrc files, GitHub Actions secrets via injected workflows, and TruffleHog (a legitimate secret scanner) repurposed to sweep the filesystem.10

Harvested material was published into newly created public GitHub repositories as five structured JSON files. The worm then found npm tokens, validated them, identified the token owner's most-downloaded packages, and republished those with itself inside. 10 Secrets were both the loot and the propagation mechanism. 1211

Business impact. Sysdig put credentials for more than 25,000 GitHub repositories in the clear. 10 GitGuardian's analysis of one compromised-machine dataset found 33,185 unique secrets across 6,943 machines. And 59 percent of those machines were CI/CD runners, not developer laptops. 1 The cost was not a breach filing; it was an enterprise-wide emergency rotation of every credential a build agent could see, with the build system frozen while it happened. 12

The lesson. Your own scanning tooling defines what an attacker who lands on a build agent can find. If a scanner can enumerate your secrets from the filesystem, so can malware. And it will, using the same tool.

Role 3 · Secret as initial access

LiteLLM / TeamPCP: an unrevoked token in the toolchain

Window
Tags rewritten 19 Mar 2026; packages published 24 Mar 2026, 10:39 UTC

Secret type

On 19 March 2026, attackers rewrote git tags in the trivy-action GitHub Action repository to point at a malicious release. LiteLLM's CI referenced that action without pinning it to a commit, so the next build pulled the poisoned version, which exfiltrated the PYPI_PUBLISH token from the GitHub Actions runner environment. 19

The attacker did not push malicious code through LiteLLM's release pipeline. They used the stolen token to upload straight to PyPI as an authenticated publisher. The maintainers are explicit: "GitHub releases only go up to v1.82.6.dev1. Versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 on PyPI were uploaded directly by the attacker."18 The official build system was bypassed, not subverted, so anyone auditing the GitHub releases would have seen nothing wrong.

The payload used a .pth file, which Python executes at interpreter startup without an explicit import, and harvested environment variables, SSH keys, AWS, GCP and Azure credentials, Kubernetes tokens and database passwords, exfiltrating to an attacker-registered domain. 18 How long it stayed live is disputed (forty minutes by the maintainers' account, about three hours by Snyk's, probably because each measures a different point between quarantine and downloads actually stopping). 19 Against roughly 3.4 million daily downloads that difference is not rhetorical.

Business impact. A vendor analysis reports exposure covering over 2,500 companies and 434,000 CI/CD pipelines. 13 That figure is single-source and uncorroborated; treat it as reported. What is not in doubt is the remediation shape: because the stolen material included publishing credentials and cloud keys, the response was not "patch the package" but rebuild environments from clean sources and rotate every credential the build process could reach.

The lesson. Three failures compounded, and only the third is about revocation. An unpinned third-party action let an attacker into the build. A long-lived publishing token sat in the runner environment where that action could read it. And the token was not rotated when the upstream Trivy compromise became known (five days elapsed between the tag rewrite and the malicious upload). The first failure is dependency hygiene; the second is credential architecture; the third is revocation latency. A programme that fixed only the third would still have lost the token.

Read the fourth row again: repository secret scanning would not have prevented any of the three. Before drawing anything from that, read the next box, because the number is a property of how these cases were chosen.

"The secret was stored somewhere" is too coarse a category to reason about coverage. Five dimensions decide whether any scanning control can act, and a control has to clear all five:

  1. Where the secret is stored. Which system holds the value.
  2. Whether the scanner can reach that surface at all.
  3. Whether the scan runs before or after exposure (prevention versus detection).
  4. Whether the result blocks the action or merely records it.
  5. Whether the credential format is detectable (part two's subject).

Section four · What to do

Build the control set in this order

The ordering principle is simple: prefer controls that make the secret worthless over controls that find it. Each rung down the ladder is a fallback for what the rung above could not cover, not an alternative to it.

Tier 1 · Highest leverage

Constrain the credential: short-lived, scoped, and bound

A credential that does not exist cannot leak. But be careful with the standard claim that a fifteen-minute token is "worth almost nothing". It is not true, and stating it that way produces bad architecture. Fifteen minutes is ample for an automated attacker to exfiltrate a dataset, create persistence, mint a second credential, stand up cloud resources, alter an access policy, or destroy something. LiteLLM's payload harvested and exfiltrated at interpreter startup; the CSA-documented agent dumped a database in under two minutes. 20

What a short lifetime actually removes is persistence and the window for replay, not initial impact. Expiry alone does not beat a sixty-second attacker; nothing in this tier does on its own. What raises the cost meaningfully is the combination: short-lived, tightly scoped, sender-constrained where possible, and continuously monitored.

Two mechanisms are routinely conflated here and do different work. Workload identity federation removes the long-lived bootstrap credential and puts issuance under policy control, so no static key sits in the runner for malware to read (the large win, and the one to chase first). What it does not do is make the token it returns unstealable: on most providers that is an ordinary bearer token. Sender-constrained credentials (mTLS-bound tokens, DPoP) are what defeat replay of the string, because using the token requires a key the thief did not get. Federation shrinks what there is to steal; binding makes what remains unusable to the thief. Neither substitutes for the other, and neither alone answers the sixty-second problem.

  • Workload identity federation for CI to cloud (OIDC-based, no stored keys in the runner). This addresses the failure mode in Shai-Hulud, where 59 percent of harvested machines were CI runners, and the one in LiteLLM, where a long-lived publishing token sat in the runner environment. Trusted publishing to package registries is the same idea.
  • Sender-constrained tokens wherever the protocol supports them. Support is patchy, so this is a "where available" control rather than a baseline.
  • Short-lived, scoped tokens for service-to-service and third-party integrations. Ask every SaaS vendor the maximum lifetime of an issued OAuth token, and treat "indefinite" as a finding.
  • Vault-brokered dynamic credentials for databases and internal services, with time-to-live in minutes.
  • Instance and pod identity instead of provisioned keys wherever the platform supports it.
  • Scope every one of the above to the narrowest permission set that works. A short-lived credential with administrative rights is a fast route to a durable one.
Tier 2 · Closes the timing gap

Automate revocation, not alerting

The measurable failure is that findings do not become revocations. Remove the human from the revocation path for the classes where you safely can.

  • Auto-revoke on detection for secret classes that meet all of four preconditions: the provider positively validated the credential as live, ownership is known and current, a replacement can be issued before the old one dies, and the failure behaviour has been tested. Revocation is a destructive action taken on the output of a detector with a known false-positive rate. Wire it to fire on unvalidated findings, or on a credential whose consumers you cannot enumerate, and you have built a self-inflicted denial-of-service that an attacker can also trigger by planting a credential-shaped string. Where the four preconditions do not hold, the automation should open the incident and start the clock, not pull the credential.
  • Where auto-revoke is too risky, auto-rotate: issue the replacement first, then invalidate the old one.
  • Set and report a rotation SLA by exposure class, owned by an engineering leader rather than the security team.
  • Wire validity checking into triage so that "still live" is a field on the ticket, not a manual step (while remembering that validity alone misclassifies a large share of critical findings).
  • Do not rebuild what the provider already does. Several providers act on a leaked token without waiting for you. Push a live Stripe key to a public repository and it is revoked within minutes, and AWS, npm, Slack and GitHub run equivalent programmes for their own formats. It is free, and it is the one path where revocation latency is already minutes. It covers only public repositories and only participating formats, so it is a supplement rather than a strategy, and provider revocation is not notification of use.
Tier 3 · Contains the blast radius

Assume the credential will be used

Design so that a stolen valid credential yields the least possible reach. This is what would have limited all three case studies.

  • Least-privilege scoping on every machine identity, reviewed on the same cycle as human access. Machine identities outnumber human ones by a wide margin and are governed far less. 2
  • Network and IP conditions on high-value credentials, so a valid token from an unexpected origin fails.
  • Anomaly detection on credential use (new region, new source ASN, unusual API surface) since valid-credential abuse produces no authentication failures to alert on.
  • Third-party integration inventory with scope and token lifetime recorded. Salesloft Drift was a marketing chatbot with a path to CRM data.
  • Egress controls on build agents, which had no legitimate reason to reach the exfiltration endpoints in either supply-chain case.
Tier 4 · Necessary, insufficient

Detect broadly, and past the repository

Scanning earns its place as the sensor that tells the tiers above where to act (and at the pre-receive boundary, as a preventive control in its own right). Deploy it at both. Do not fund the detective part as if it were the preventive one.

  • Server-side push protection first. The enforcement boundary: it binds every push from every clone, and logs both blocks and bypasses. Require a bypass justification that generates a reviewed record.
  • Local hooks second, as defence in depth. User-installed and removable with --no-verify: they reduce load on the boundary above and evidence nothing to an assessor.
  • Full git history, not just HEAD, and re-scan any repository the moment its visibility changes.
  • Extend coverage beyond repositories to Slack, Jira, Confluence, ticket systems, wikis, container images, IaC state files, MCP configuration files, and CI logs.
  • Explicitly govern AI coding assistants: block secret patterns in generated output, and sample assistant-authored commits as a separate population. The evidence is one assistant on one vendor's telemetry (enough to measure it in your own estate, not enough to price it as higher-risk).
What this ladder costs, which it has not so far said

The ordering above is by risk reduction per control, and that is not the same as by feasibility. Read honestly, the top tiers are the expensive ones: workload identity federation, sender-constrained tokens, automated rotation and discovery across non-repository surfaces are multi-quarter programmes, not configuration changes. They carry migration cost across every consumer of every credential, availability risk during cutover, ownership disputes between platform, security and application teams over who holds the rotation runbook, and a real chance of a production incident caused by the remediation rather than by the exposure.

Tier four (detection) is last on leverage and first on tractability. It can be switched on this quarter by a small team, and it produces evidence immediately. That is a legitimate reason to start there, and it is why most programmes do. The failure is not starting with detection. It is stopping there, and reporting detection volume as though it were risk reduction.

A sequencing that respects both: stand up the server-side push gate now, because it is cheap and genuinely preventive; use what it finds to build the credential inventory you will need for anything else; and spend the tier-one budget on the classes that inventory shows actually matter, rather than on a federation programme scoped by ambition.

Measure the right things

Most secrets programmes report detection volume, which is a measure of how much code was written, not how much risk was removed. Three metrics are worth putting in front of a risk committee:

  • Mean time to revocation, from detection to confirmed invalidation, segmented by exposure class. This is the number that maps to the sixty-second problem.
  • Percentage of findings still valid at 30 days. If your figure resembles the industry's 64 percent, detection spend is not converting into risk reduction.
  • Percentage of machine identities with a bounded lifetime. This is the leading indicator; it is the only metric that improves the other two structurally rather than through effort.

A supporting fourth, if you have supply-chain exposure: time to complete a full credential rotation across the CI estate, measured by exercise rather than estimate. Both supply-chain cases above turned on that number, and most organisations do not know theirs.

Chapter 1

The Sixty-Second Credential

Assumptions, sourcing, and confidence

Established. Incident facts for Salesloft Drift and Shai-Hulud 2.0 are corroborated across vendor research, vendor incident guidance and mainstream security press. The CISA advisory cited covers the earlier September 2025 npm campaign and is background rather than corroboration for the November events. Breach economics and initial-access shares come from the 2026 IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report and the 2026 Verizon DBIR.

Single-source or vendor-sourced. Secret volume, validity persistence, AI-service leak growth and MCP configuration figures derive predominantly from GitGuardian's annual research; GitGuardian sells secret detection. The 27-day remediation figure is from a GitGuardian/CyberArk survey, self-reported by IT decision-makers rather than measured. LiteLLM scale figures (2,500+ companies, 434,000 pipelines) remain single-source from CloudSEK and uncorroborated. The sub-sixty-second median exploitation figure is industry reporting, corroborated in order of magnitude by independent honeypot research.

Contested and superseded. The LiteLLM exposure window is genuinely disputed: 40 minutes per the maintainers, roughly three hours per Snyk's reconstruction. Widely circulated accounts stating that LiteLLM's build system produced the malicious releases are superseded by the maintainers' own record, which shows GitHub releases stopping at v1.82.6.dev1 and the packages uploaded directly with a stolen token.

Asserted rather than demonstrated. The claim that attackers use language models to classify harvested credentials by validity, privilege and value is widely repeated but I found no primary source documenting it as an observed capability. It is labelled as a characterisation in section two. What is documented is automated harvesting and validation at scale, and LLM agents operating at machine speed post-exploitation.

Inference, flagged. The per-commit leak-rate calculation (approximately 1.6 percent to 1.5 percent) is derived here from published aggregates for secret count, commit count and developer growth. It is not a reported statistic and carries the compounding uncertainty of three separate figures.

Assumption. Guidance assumes an enterprise with cloud infrastructure, an active CI/CD estate, and third-party SaaS integrations. Organisations with predominantly on-premises, low-change environments should weight tier 3 containment above tier 1 elimination.

Chapter 2

Nothing Catches Everything

Assumptions, sourcing, and confidence

Established. Precision and recall figures are from a peer-reviewed ESEM 2023 study with a manually labelled corpus. Licences, pricing and tier boundaries were verified against each project's own repository, licence file or vendor documentation as of August 2026.

Explicitly unverified (check before relying on it. Whether GitGuardian's free Starter plan permits ggshield in CI (two primary sources conflict)). Snyk free-tier limits and whether Snyk Secrets is included. Whether Bitbucket Cloud has native, non-Pipelines secret scanning and on which plan. The exact published rule counts for Gitleaks, Betterleaks and Kingfisher, none of which publish a figure. The maintenance status of git-secrets, which is not archived but appears dormant.

Dated. The ESEM 2023 numbers predate Betterleaks, Kingfisher, Titus and every AI-service credential format. Treat them as evidence of how these tools fail, not as a current league table.

Limitations of the benchmark in section four. One run, one seed, 39 planted secrets, 18 decoys and 6 unscored. That is enough to measure recall attributably and far too little to measure precision meaningfully. A real repository's noise floor is orders of magnitude larger, which is why five of six tools score above 90 percent precision here and between 1 and 75 percent on 818 real repositories. The checksum experiment is planted but not broken out in the published scoring. Rulesets drift monthly, so any snapshot ages quickly. Treat the run as a reproducible measurement of one corpus, not as a league table.

Known defect in the corpus, unfixed at the time of writing. Four planted "secrets" are AWS access-key IDs, which cannot authenticate on their own and should not be scored as credentials. Section four gives the recomputed figures; the ranking flips, no tool retains a unique catch, and the case for layering weakens to a single secret. The adjusted numbers are my arithmetic on the published per-tool totals, not a re-run. Detect-secrets could not be recomputed at all, because the published summary does not say which side of the identifier split it fell on. Until the corpus reclassifies identifiers as locator signals and the run is repeated, treat every recall figure in this section as provisional.

Disclosure. Betterleaks development is funded by Aikido, which sells a competing commercial product. GitGuardian research is cited throughout and GitGuardian sells secret detection.

Chapter 3

The Governed Pipeline

Assumptions, sourcing, and confidence

Reproducible. Every configuration, workflow and test in sections three and four is in the repository and runs in CI. The regression suite's result (21 scenarios, 16 blocked, 5 passed, 0 deviations) is reproducible with python regression/run.py against Gitleaks 8.30.1. The two gaps it found on first run, and the precision defect in the first version of the connection-string rule, are recorded in the commit history rather than reconstructed.

Directly observed. The hosted push-protection result in section five was produced by an actual push at this repository on 30 August 2026, with the alert retrieved from the alert API and only sanitised metadata retained. The API returns a secret field, which is deliberately not copied. The block, the bypass, the retry and the alert are all this repository's own result, reproducible by the procedure published alongside it. An earlier run of the same experiment in the predecessor repository is retained as provenance.

Judgement, not measurement. The rollout order in section one, the six-column control specification in section two, and the split of tools between gating and discovery are engineering positions. They are defensible from the evidence in parts one and two, but they are not measurements and another team could order them differently with good reasons. Particularly an organisation whose host offers no push gate at all, where the CI gate has to carry weight it is not designed for.

Out of scope. Section seven names the runtime-credential surface and the architectural options that shrink the static one. It does not evaluate any of them. Workload identity, sender-constrained tokens and runtime telemetry each deserve their own treatment, and nothing here should be read as an implementation recommendation for a specific product.

Platform specificity. The reference implementation is GitHub Actions. The four decisions in section three (three-outcome exit handling, verified redaction, full history, pinned and checksummed tools) are host-independent. The syntax is not.

Chapter 4

The Unnamed Control

Assumptions, sourcing, and confidence

Verified against primary sources. NIST SP 800-218 v1.1 and SP 800-218A practice text (NIST PDFs); OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 requirement text (project repository); CIS Controls v8.1 Control 16 safeguards (CIS Assessment Specification); PCI DSS future-dated requirement effective dates (PCI SSC Summary of Changes); DORA RTS 2024/1774 article text (EUR-Lex); FTC and SEC complaint paragraphs (ftc.gov, sec.gov).

Verified against secondary sources only. Check before quoting verbatim. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control body text (the standard is paywalled; control numbers and titles are corroborated across multiple sources, normative text is not). SOC 2 criteria other than CC6.1 and CC8.1 (the AICPA TSC document is gated). PCI DSS full requirement text (the PCI SSC standard is gated; text taken from a QSA-published matrix reproducing v4.0.1). The effective dates are from the public PCI SSC Summary of Changes. MAS TRM paragraph numbering (mas.gov.sg blocks automated retrieval; chapter structure matches the published contents). DORA Article 9(4) paragraph lettering.

Not found. No published push protection bypass rate exists from any vendor or academic source. No ICO, NYDFS or HHS OCR enforcement action naming hardcoded credentials as a cited cause was located. Both absences are stated in the article rather than filled.

Vendor-sourced. The 27-day remediation figure, the 79 percent leak incidence and the 75/44 percent confidence gap come from a survey sponsored by GitGuardian and CyberArk, both of whom sell in this market, and the respondents were IT decision-makers rather than developers. Treated as directional and labelled as such in the text.

Assumption. This assumes an organisation with a git-based SDLC, CI/CD, and an external audit or regulatory obligation. Organisations without an assessment obligation should read section four as a design reference rather than a requirements list.

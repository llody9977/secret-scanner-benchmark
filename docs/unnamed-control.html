The Unnamed Control

No framework requires you to operate a thing called a secret scanner. Three name hardcoded secrets in normative text, each with a scope limit that matters; the rest reach the control by inference. That gap explains most of what goes wrong in governing it.

Part 4 of 4 · Governance Research current to August 2026 ~4,600 words · 21 min

Executive summary

  1. No framework in this set requires an organisation to buy or operate a product called a "secret scanner." They require outcomes (secrets not hardcoded, secrets managed securely, source and deployment artifacts reviewed, plaintext secrets monitored, credentials rotated and protected, controls evidenced). Automated scanning is one implementation of those outcomes and one way to evidence them. Reading a scanner requirement into a framework that requires an outcome is the most common error in this space, and it cuts both ways: it oversells the tool and it lets an organisation think a purchase discharged an obligation.
  2. Three frameworks name hardcoded secrets in normative text, and each carries a scope limit that is usually dropped in the retelling: PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 8.6.2 (mandatory since 31 March 2025, but bounded to application and system accounts capable of interactive login, not every API key or machine credential); OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 requirement 13.3.1 (explicit and unambiguous, but ASVS binds you only if you adopt it, and it is not itself a regulatory obligation); and NIST SP 800-218A ("continuously monitor each environment for plaintext secrets", the most explicit line in the family but scoped to generative-AI and dual-use environments). Everything else (ISO 27001, SOC 2, base NIST SSDF, CIS, DORA) reaches the control by inference. 12
  3. That inference gap is not academic. It is why secret scanning is funded inconsistently, owned ambiguously, and evidenced badly. An auditor will map it to SOC 2 CC6.1, a criterion that never mentions secrets. 3
  4. Where you install the scanner determines what you can claim. Server-side pre-receive push protection is the control to lead with. It is the only preventive placement that applies to every push regardless of local setup and cannot be removed by the developer it constrains. Local hooks are defence in depth; everything downstream of the push is detective, and should be described that way in a control narrative.
  5. Governance routinely measures the wrong thing. Detection volume rises with headcount. GitGuardian and CyberArk's practitioner survey found average time to remediate a leaked secret was 27 days. That is the number that maps to risk. 4
  6. The friction is real and measured. Precision across nine tools ranged from 1 to 75 percent in peer-reviewed testing;5 a USENIX study found developers "often bypass prevention approaches when workflow changes create friction," citing excessive false positives directly. 6
  7. The same survey found 79 percent of organisations had experienced a secrets leak, while 75 percent of respondents expressed strong confidence in their secrets management (and reported only 44 percent of developers following best practice). 4 Confidence is not the control.
  8. There is enforcement precedent. The FTC's complaint against Uber describes a plaintext AWS access key posted to GitHub as the proximate cause of two breaches. And expressly notes the second key was in a private repository. 7
  9. Governance that works looks like: named owners per credential class, a rotation SLA by exposure class, a friction budget the security team spends deliberately, and metrics reported on revocation rather than detection.
Section one · Placement

Where the scanner sits decides what you can claim

"We have secret scanning" is not a control statement. It does not say what is prevented, what is merely observed, who feels the cost, or what evidence exists. Six placements are available, and they differ on all four.

The vocabulary: pre-commit and pre-receive scanning are preventive; post-commit scanning is detective; validity checking is prioritisation; revocation and rotation are corrective; and short-lived, scoped, workload-bound identity is preventive architecture sitting outside this pipeline altogether. Using those words precisely in a control narrative is most of what separates an assertion an assessor accepts from one they probe.

The diagram numbers the placements in pipeline order, and those numbers are local to the diagram. Across this series control points are cited by name, because each article orders them differently. Deployment priority runs differently again, and the difference matters. Start at the server-side push gate: it is the only preventive placement that binds every push from every clone, cannot be removed by the developer it constrains, and writes its own audit trail. Then the pull-request CI gate and the scheduled history scan for coverage and history. Only then the IDE and pre-commit points, which are genuinely useful (the earliest feedback is the cheapest to act on) but which are opt-in, locally bypassable with --no-verify, and absent from every fresh clone until someone installs them. A programme that rolls out pre-commit hooks and calls the control implemented has enforced nothing.

The governance conclusion from that table is correspondingly narrow. The push gate, the pull-request CI gate and the scheduled history scan are the ones you can evidence to an auditor. The IDE and pre-commit points reduce the volume reaching the others, but a control narrative resting on them is resting on developer goodwill and a local config file. Non-repository discovery is the one almost nobody deploys and the one that covers the incident class repository scanning structurally cannot see.

Section two · Effectiveness

What having a scanner does and does not prove

A deployed scanner proves coverage. It does not prove control. The distinction matters because most secrets programmes report the first and imply the second.

Remediation lag 27days Average time to remediate a leaked secret, across 1,000 IT decision-makers surveyed. 4
Experienced a leak 79% Of organisations surveyed; 77 percent of those incidents caused tangible damage. 4
Confidence gap 75/ 44% Strongly confident in their secrets management, against 44 percent of developers reported as following best practice. 4

Read the third tile carefully, including its weakness. It is a vendor-sponsored survey of IT decision-makers, not of developers, so the 44 percent figure is a manager's report about their engineers rather than a measurement of engineering behaviour. That does not make it useless. A thirty-one point gap between how well leaders think this is going and how well they think their own developers are doing it is a governance signal in itself, whichever number is wrong.

The same survey found organisations running an average of six distinct secrets manager instances, and dedicating 32.4 percent of security budgets to secrets management and code security. 4 Substantial spend, fragmented tooling, and a 27-day remediation lag is a recognisable pattern: the control is bought but not operated.

The four claims governance usually makes, ranked by how defensible they are

Defensible

"Every repository in scope is scanned, and we can show which." This is a coverage assertion, it is measurable, and a scheduled full scan produces the evidence. Make sure "in scope" is defined by an inventory rather than by which repositories happened to have a workflow file.

Defensible

"Secrets matching known patterns are blocked at push, and bypasses are logged and reviewed." Server-side enforcement with an audit trail. This is the strongest single claim available. It is bounded by "known patterns". Do not drop that qualifier.

Weak

"We have no secrets in our code." Unsupportable. The best measured recall in peer-reviewed testing was 88 percent, and that tool was wrong on more than half its findings. 5 The honest form is "we detect no secrets of the types our tooling covers," which is a materially smaller statement.

Weak

"Findings are remediated." Only if you measure revocation rather than ticket closure. GitGuardian found 64 percent of secrets valid in 2022 still valid in January 2026;8 many of those had tickets. A closed ticket and a revoked credential are different facts and belong in different columns.

Metrics that belong in a risk committee pack

  • Coverage: percentage of repositories in the inventory scanned in the last 30 days, and percentage with push protection enforced.
  • Mean time to revocation by exposure class (from detection to confirmed invalidation at the provider, not to ticket closure).
  • Findings still valid at 30 days. The single best proxy for whether detection is converting into risk reduction.
  • Bypass rate and bypass review latency for push protection, with the justification text sampled quarterly.
  • Percentage of machine identities with a bounded lifetime. The leading indicator; the only one that improves the others structurally.
  • Precision, measured not assumed: triage a random sample of 50 findings per quarter and report what proportion were real. If nobody has ever measured this, that is the first thing to fix.

Detection volume can stay in the pack, but label it as an exposure indicator rather than a performance one. It rises when you hire engineers and falls when you cut scope, and neither movement says anything about control effectiveness.

Section three · Friction

What this costs the people who live with it

Security functions routinely underestimate this, and the underestimate is why controls degrade. The friction is measurable and the mechanism by which it defeats the control is documented.

The false positive tax

Precision across the nine tools in the ESEM 2023 study ranged from 1 to 75 percent, with five of nine scoring under 7 percent. 5 A tool at 6 percent precision produces roughly sixteen false alarms for every real finding. That is not a tuning problem to be solved next quarter; it is the operating condition for a large part of the tool market.

One qualification, because it cuts both ways. A 2026 run of six current tools against a purpose-built synthetic corpus put five of the six above 90 percent precision. 21 That is not evidence the problem is solved. A corpus with eighteen decoys has almost none of the noise a real repository carries, and the same run's author says so explicitly. The governance point is the one that survives both datasets: precision is a property of a tool meeting a particular codebase, not a property of the tool. Neither published figure tells you your number. Measure it, on your repositories, and report it.

Vendors acknowledge it in their own framing. GitHub, reporting a 75.76 percent reduction in false positives in June 2026, described the problem directly: "too many false positives make alerts harder to trust," and "when alerts feel noisy, developers spend more time triaging and less time fixing real issues."9 That is a relative improvement against their own prior baseline, not an absolute rate (no vendor publishes one).

What developers actually do about it

The best evidence here is Krause and colleagues' mixed-methods study presented at USENIX Security 2023, which surveyed 109 developers and interviewed a subset about how they handle secrets in repositories. 6 Four findings matter for governance:

  • 30.3 percent had personally experienced a secret leakage incident. This is not a hypothetical control for the population being governed.
  • Developers reported that secret scanners "produce excessive false positives, requiring manual review and causing adoption resistance." Named directly, by the people using them.
  • "Developers often bypass prevention approaches when workflow changes create friction." The bypass is a response to friction, not to disagreement with the goal.
  • Maintenance overhead was cited by half the interview participants as a barrier to adopting secret management tooling, alongside time and cost, and poor documentation for open-source options.

This is consistent with a decade-old and still-unrefuted finding about security tooling generally: Johnson and colleagues at ICSE 2013 found developers abandoned static analysis tools over warning volume and results that "lack enough information to help developers on what the problem is, why it is a problem, what they should be doing different," and strongly preferred tools that run in the background over tools that require stopping work. 10 That study is qualitative; do not attach percentages to it. Its structural point has held for thirteen years.

A gap worth naming: nobody publishes bypass rates

Push protection bypass telemetry exists. GitHub has shipped organisation-level push protection metrics since 2023 and bypass-request metrics since 2024, so the number sits inside every GitHub Enterprise organisation that has it enabled. 11 No aggregate figure has ever been published, by any vendor, and I could find no academic study measuring it.

That absence is a governance finding rather than a research gap. The most direct available measure of whether a preventive control is actually preventing (how often people override it) is collected everywhere and reported nowhere. If you run this control, you can read your own number this week, and you should, because there is no benchmark to compare it against and no one else is going to produce one.

Spending the friction budget deliberately

Treat developer tolerance as a finite resource allocated on purpose, not consumed by default. Five rules that follow from the evidence above:

  1. Block on high-precision detections only. Structured, checksum-validated provider tokens block the push. Generic high-entropy findings warn or route to a queue. Blocking on a 6-percent-precision rule trains developers to bypass on reflex.
  2. Make the fix easier than the bypass. The block message should name the credential type, the exact line, the approved storage location, and the command to rotate it. A message that says only "secret detected" converts every block into a support ticket.
  3. Give bypass a real path. A justified, logged, reviewed bypass is a control. A bypass that requires filing an exception with a security team on a two-day SLA is a control that will be routed around, and you will lose the telemetry as well as the secret.
  4. Own the baseline centrally, review it in code. Allowlists should live in the repository, be visible in code review, and expire. Ungoverned allowlists are how a tool quietly stops detecting anything.
  5. Publish your own precision number to engineering. Nothing rebuilds trust in a noisy control faster than the security team measuring and reporting the noise itself, then acting on it.
Section four · Obligation

Which standards actually require this

Here is the finding that gives this article its title. Across eight frameworks that an enterprise is plausibly assessed against, three name hardcoded secrets in normative text. Not one of them names a scanner. Every framework here requires an outcome: secrets not hardcoded, secrets managed for their lifecycle, source and deployment artifacts reviewed, plaintext secrets monitored, credentials rotated. Automated scanning is an implementation choice and an evidence mechanism, not the obligation. The three explicit ones are also each scope-limited (PCI to interactively-loggable accounts, ASVS to organisations that have adopted ASVS, SP 800-218A to AI and dual-use environments) so "explicit" is not the same as "applies to you". The rest reach the control by inference. Which is entirely valid for an auditor, and entirely useless for a security leader trying to get it funded on a compliance argument.

Three things to get right when you cite these

  1. PCI 8.6.2 has a scoping limit that is routinely misstated. Requirements 8.6.1 and 8.6.2 apply to application and system accounts "that can be used for interactive login." 8.6.3, the rotation requirement, applies to all application and system accounts regardless. A machine credential that cannot interactively log in is out of scope for the no-hardcoding rule and in scope for the rotation rule. 18 Claiming 8.6.2 covers every service credential is the most common error made about this clause, and a QSA will correct it.
  2. Do not claim the base NIST SSDF mandates secret scanning. SP 800-218 v1.1 contains no practice or task whose text names secrets, hardcoded credentials, passwords or cryptographic keys. The mapping runs through PW.5.1 and PW.7.2 by implication. The AI profile, SP 800-218A, is where it becomes explicit. And citing the AI profile for a non-AI system is a stretch an assessor may not accept.
  3. SOC 2 has no clause that names secrets, and that is the useful observation. A control that essentially every organisation is tested on, that no criterion names, will be scoped by whatever the auditor's mapping happens to be. Define the mapping yourself, in your control narrative, before someone else defines it for you.

Enforcement precedent, honestly stated

The standards record is stronger than the enforcement record. Two cases are worth knowing, and the second comes with a caveat that must not be dropped.

  • FTC v. Uber Technologies (Docket C-4662, revised complaint 2018). The Commission's complaint describes an intruder accessing consumers' personal information in an S3 datastore "using an access key that one of Respondent's engineers had publicly posted to GitHub," and a second incident where "the key was in plain text in code that was posted to a private GitHub repository," reached using passwords exposed in unrelated breaches. 7 This is a federal regulator, in a complaint that produced a consent order, treating a plaintext key in source code as the proximate cause. And the private-repository detail defeats the "it is only an internal repo" argument directly. It also aligns with GitGuardian's finding that internal repositories are roughly six times more likely to contain hardcoded secrets than public ones. 8
  • SEC v. SolarWinds and Brown (S.D.N.Y., filed October 2023). The complaint quotes a researcher's 2019 notification that a public GitHub repository was "leaking ftp credential belong[ing] to SolarWinds," with the password solarwinds123.19 The caveat is essential: in mid-2024 the court dismissed most of the SEC's claims.20 Cite it as an example of a securities regulator treating a leaked repository credential as material to investor disclosure. Do not cite it as a finding of liability.

I searched for ICO, NYDFS and HHS OCR actions naming hardcoded credentials as a cited cause and found none. The honest position for a governance paper is that Uber is the leading precedent, SolarWinds is the leading contested one, and the enforcement record is thinner than the standards record. Build the case on obligation and incident evidence rather than on fear of a fine.

Section five · Operating model

Governance that survives contact with engineering

Element 1 · Ownership

Name an owner per credential class, not per finding

The failure mode in every programme that stalls is findings landing in a queue with no owner. Ownership should attach to the credential class and the issuing system, not to whoever last touched the file.

  • Cloud credentials, CI credentials, database credentials, third-party integration tokens: each gets a named accountable engineering leader.
  • Security owns the detection pipeline and the standard. Engineering owns revocation and the SLA. Do not let those blur. A security team that owns rotation becomes the bottleneck it was meant to remove.
  • Third-party integration tokens need an owner at onboarding. Salesloft Drift was a marketing tool with a path to CRM data; the owner was marketing, and nobody had recorded that.
Element 2 · Policy

Write a standard that can be tested, not a principle

"Secrets shall not be stored in source code" is unfalsifiable as written and unenforceable in practice. A testable standard names the artefacts, the approved alternatives, and the deadline. That is exactly what PCI 8.6.2 does and why it is the strongest clause in the corpus.

  • Enumerate the artefacts in scope: source files, configuration and property files, scripts, IaC, container images and layers, CI definitions and logs, notebooks, ticket and wiki content.
  • Name the approved storage for each credential class, and make the approved path the easy one.
  • State the rotation SLA per exposure class, with the owner.
  • Define the exception process, its maximum duration, and who reviews expiry.
  • State the credential architecture standard alongside the storage rule: maximum lifetime, required scoping, and whether the credential must be workload-bound. A standard that only says "do not hardcode" leaves the long-lived unscoped token as a compliant outcome.

Element 3 · Evidence

Assemble the audit pack before the audit

The evidence that satisfies CC6.1, ISO A.5.17, PCI 8.6.2 and DORA RTS Art. 16(3) is largely the same evidence. Collect it once, map it many times.

  • Repository inventory with scan coverage and last-scan date per repository.
  • Push protection enforcement status per organisation, plus bypass log with justifications and review records.
  • Findings register with detection timestamp, revocation timestamp, and current validity. The revocation timestamp is the one auditors increasingly ask for.
  • The secure coding standard itself, with evidence of developer acknowledgement (PCI 6.2.2 requires annual training for development personnel).
  • Exception register with expiry dates and review evidence.
  • Your own measured precision sample, which turns "we have a tool" into "we know how well the tool works."
Element 4 · Reporting

Report on revocation, escalate on lifetime

The risk committee needs two things from this control: whether exposed credentials are being invalidated, and whether the population of long-lived credentials is shrinking. Everything else is operational detail.

  • Report mean time to revocation and findings-still-valid-at-30-days every cycle. Segment by exposure class.
  • Report percentage of machine identities with a bounded lifetime as the leading indicator, and treat a flat line as the escalation trigger.
  • Report push protection bypass rate with sampled justifications. The number nobody publishes, that you can read from your own tenant.
  • Keep detection volume in the pack, labelled as an exposure indicator. It tracks engineering headcount, not control performance.
Closing the series

Secret scanning is a detection control that most frameworks require without naming, that most organisations buy without operating, and that no tool performs completely. None of those three facts is a reason not to run it.

They are reasons to describe it accurately: preventive at the pre-receive boundary and detective everywhere downstream, a sensor that tells a revocation pipeline where to act, funded on the strength of that pipeline rather than on detector counts, evidenced at the three placements that produce an audit trail, and paid for in developer friction that is spent deliberately rather than consumed by accident.

And it is worth resisting the tidy closing line this subject invites. The credential that never existed is the only unambiguous win. A short-lived one is not a win by itself. Fifteen minutes is ample for an automated attacker to take data, create persistence, or mint a longer-lived credential. What genuinely reduces risk is the combination: short-lived, tightly scoped, sender-constrained where the protocol allows, and monitored in use. Note that workload identity federation and sender constraint are different controls. Federation removes the standing credential, while mTLS or DPoP binding is what stops a stolen token from being replayed. Governance that asks "are our credentials short-lived?" has asked a quarter of the question.
